# 1 Materials and Methods

## 1.1 Materials

### 1.1.1 Bacterial strains

*Bacillus megaterium* CCTCC M2021167, obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC), was used as the starting strain and designated But-0. A series of engineered strains, But-1 through But-6, was sequentially constructed from But-0 by deleting host genes in competing metabolic pathways and integrating heterologous butyrate biosynthetic genes into the corresponding chromosomal loci. Using engineered strain But-6 as the parent, three successive rounds of mutagenesis—atmospheric and room-temperature plasma (ARTP), ultraviolet (UV), and ARTP—were conducted to obtain the mutant strains But-6A100, But-6B100, and But-6C100, respectively. The characteristics and sources of all strains used in this study are listed in Table 1.

## 1.2 Culture Media and Cultivation Conditions

### 1.2.1 Seed media

The solid seed medium used for strain activation contained (g/L): glucose, 30.0; yeast extract, 5.0; peptone, 5.0; and agar, 15.0. The liquid seed medium contained (g/L): glucose, 30.0; yeast extract, 5.0; and peptone, 5.0. Both media were prepared at natural pH and autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min (glucose was prepared and autoclaved separately, then aseptically mixed with the other components after cooling prior to inoculation). Luria–Bertani (LB) medium used for plasmid construction and preparation of mutagenized cell suspensions contained (g/L): peptone, 10.0; yeast extract, 5.0; and NaCl, 10.0 (with 15.0 g/L agar added for solid LB medium).

### 1.2.2 Fermentation medium

The basal liquid fermentation medium contained (g/L): glucose, 30.0; yeast extract, 5.0; and peptone, 5.0. The medium was used at its natural pH and autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min, with glucose sterilized separately and aseptically mixed with the other components before use.

### 1.2.3 Seed cultivation

Preserved strains were streaked onto solid activation medium and incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. A single colony was inoculated into a 100 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 25 mL of liquid seed medium and cultivated at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 16 h until reaching the logarithmic growth phase to serve as the seed culture.

### 1.2.4 Shake-flask fermentation

The seed culture was inoculated at 1% (v/v) into a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 100 mL of fermentation medium and cultivated at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 72 h. At the end of fermentation, samples were taken to measure the optical density at 600 nm ($\text{OD}_{600}$) and the concentrations of butyrate, lactate, acetate, and succinate in the fermentation broth.

## 1.3 Construction of Engineered Strains

### 1.3.1 Preparation of homologous recombination donor DNA fragments

Homologous recombination donor DNA fragments were constructed according to the target loci and integrated genes listed in Table 1. Taking the construction of engineered strain But-1 as an example, the upstream and downstream homology arms of the *ldhA* locus were amplified by PCR using the genomic DNA of the starting strain But-0 as the template, and the target gene fragment was amplified from a template carrying the *E. coli*-derived *atoB*$^{\mathrm{Ec}}$ gene. After ligating the target gene with expression regulatory elements including a strong promoter and a terminator, the upstream homology arm, the *atoB*$^{\mathrm{Ec}}$ expression cassette, and the downstream homology arm were assembled into a complete homologous recombination donor DNA fragment via overlap-extension PCR (fusion PCR). Donor DNA fragments for the remaining target loci were prepared using the same method.

### 1.3.2 Sequential construction of engineered strains

Chromosomal gene modifications were performed using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homologous recombination. The constructed donor DNA fragment was co-electroporated into competent host cells along with the pTargetF plasmid (carrying the sgRNA targeting the specific locus) and the pCas plasmid to achieve homologous recombination repair at the target site. Starting from But-0, the *ldhA* gene was deleted and replaced with *atoB*$^{\mathrm{Ec}}$ at the same locus to obtain strain But-1; subsequently, using But-1 as the parent, the *ackA* gene was deleted and replaced with *hbd*$^{\mathrm{Ca}}$ at the same locus ($\Delta ackA::hbd^{\mathrm{Ca}}$) to obtain strain But-2. Following the same procedure, the modifications $\Delta adhE::crt^{\mathrm{Ca}}$, $\Delta pflB::ter^{\mathrm{Ca}}$, and $\Delta frdABCD::tesB^{\mathrm{Ec}}$ were sequentially introduced to generate engineered strains But-3, But-4, and But-5, respectively. Finally, based on strain But-5, five additional gene deletion-and-integration modifications—$\Delta poxB::atoB^{\mathrm{Ec}}$, $\Delta pta::hbd^{\mathrm{Ca}}$, $\Delta alaT::crt^{\mathrm{Ca}}$, $\Delta ppc::ter^{\mathrm{Td}}$, and $\Delta mgsA::tesB^{\mathrm{Ec}}$—were sequentially completed to obtain engineered strain But-6.

### 1.3.3 Screening and verification of recombinant strains

Following electroporation and recovery, cell suspensions were spread onto selective agar plates containing the appropriate antibiotics. Single colonies were picked as candidate transformants and screened by colony PCR using verification primers located outside the homology arms of the target locus, combined with agarose gel electrophoresis and DNA sequencing to confirm gene deletion and heterologous gene integration. Verified positive clones were subjected to induction and cultivation at an elevated temperature to cure the editing plasmids pTargetF and pCas. After re-verifying the target locus sequence, the strains were preserved in glycerol stocks for the next round of genetic modification.

## 1.4 Sequential ARTP–UV–ARTP Mutagenesis and Strain Screening

### 1.4.1 Preparation of cell suspensions for mutagenesis

Strains But-6, But-6A100, and But-6B100 served as the parental strains for the three successive rounds of mutagenesis, respectively. Each parental strain was streaked from a glycerol stock onto an LB agar plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A fresh single colony was inoculated into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of liquid LB medium and cultured at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 10 h. The culture was centrifuged at 6,000 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended in sterile physiological saline, and counted using a bacterial counting chamber to adjust the cell concentration to approximately $1 \times 10^8$ CFU/mL prior to use.

### 1.4.2 ARTP and UV mutagenesis procedures

In the first round, starting strain But-6 was subjected to ARTP mutagenesis (Fig. 2A). A 10 μL aliquot of the prepared cell suspension was evenly spotted onto the center of a sterilized metal slide and placed in the ARTP mutagenesis apparatus. High-purity nitrogen was used as the working gas, with the radiofrequency power set at 120 W, treatment temperature at room temperature, gas flow rate at 10 SLPM, distance between the plasma emitter and sample at 5 mm, and exposure time of 30 s. Immediately after treatment, the metal slide was transferred into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 990 μL of sterile liquid seed medium and vortexed for 100 s to elute the cells. The eluate was then transferred into a 500 mL shake flask containing 50 mL of liquid seed medium and incubated at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 12 h. After appropriate serial dilution, 100 μL of the culture was spread onto solid medium plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, from which single colonies were picked as candidate mutants. In the second round, the high-yielding mutant But-6A100 selected from the first round was used as the parent for UV mutagenesis: 5 mL of the prepared cell suspension was placed in a sterile Petri dish (9 cm diameter) and irradiated for 30 s directly beneath a 30 W UV lamp at a distance of 30 cm; the treated suspension was serially diluted and plated in the dark throughout the process. In the third round, the selected mutant But-6B100 from the second round served as the parent for the second ARTP mutagenesis, following the same treatment parameters and cell recovery procedures as the first ARTP round.

### 1.4.3 Primary screening and shake-flask validation

After each round of mutagenesis, 100 candidate mutants were randomly picked from the plates (alongside one corresponding parental strain as a control) and inoculated into test tubes containing liquid fermentation medium for primary screening to determine butyrate titers. Candidates exhibiting a higher butyrate titer than the corresponding parental control were defined as positive mutants. The highest-producing mutants from the first two rounds, But-6A100 and But-6B100, served as the parents for the subsequent rounds, while But-6C100 was retained as the final selected mutant.

The starting strain But-6 and the three selected mutants (But-6A100, But-6B100, and But-6C100) were independently validated by shake-flask fermentation following the procedures described in Sections 1.2.3 and 1.2.4. Butyrate titers and $\text{OD}_{600}$ were measured at the end of fermentation, with three independent biological replicates performed for each strain.

## 1.5 Optimization of Fermentation Medium Components

Using mutant strain But-6C100 as the test strain and the basal fermentation medium (containing 30.0 g/L glucose) as the starting point, sequential single-factor experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of varying concentrations of yeast extract, peptone, corn steep liquor, and molasses on butyrate production (the optimal concentration determined for each preceding component was maintained as the baseline condition for the next component). Yeast extract, peptone, and molasses were each tested at four concentration gradients (2.5, 5.0, 7.5, and 10.0 g/L), whereas corn steep liquor was tested at five concentration gradients (4.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, and 12.0 g/L). All treatment groups were cultivated in shake flasks as described in Section 1.2.4, and butyrate titers were measured at the end of fermentation, with three biological replicates per treatment.

## 1.6 Fed-Batch Fermentation in a 5 L Bioreactor

Based on the single-factor optimization results, fed-batch fermentation of mutant strain But-6C100 was carried out in a 5 L stirred-tank bioreactor using the optimized medium components (7.5 g/L yeast extract, 5.0 g/L peptone, 10.0 g/L corn steep liquor, and 7.5 g/L molasses) (Fig. 4A). Following plate activation and seed cultivation, the culture was inoculated into the 5 L bioreactor at an initial $\text{OD}_{600}$ of 0.5. The working volume of the bioreactor was 2.5 L with an initial glucose concentration of 50.0 g/L, and cultivation was conducted at 37 °C with an agitation speed of 300 rpm and an aeration rate of 1.0 vvm. During fermentation, the broth pH was maintained at 7.0 by automated feeding of 4 mol/L NaOH solution. When the initial glucose was nearly exhausted at 24 h, a concentrated glucose solution was continuously fed using a feeding pump to maintain the residual glucose concentration within the target feeding window of 1–5 g/L. The fermentation lasted for 48 h, during which samples were aseptically withdrawn every 6 h to determine $\text{OD}_{600}$, residual glucose concentration, and butyrate titer.

## 1.7 Analytical Methods

Determination of $\text{OD}_{600}$: Fermentation broth was appropriately diluted, and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm using a visible spectrophotometer. Determination of residual glucose concentration: The supernatant obtained by centrifuging the fermentation broth was diluted 100-fold and analyzed using an SBA-40D biosensor analyzer.

Determination of organic acid concentrations: The concentrations of butyrate, lactate, acetate, and succinate in the fermentation broth were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Fermentation broth samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the collected supernatants were filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane prior to injection. Chromatographic conditions were as follows: a Bio-Rad Aminex HPX-87H organic acid analysis column (300 mm × 7.8 mm); mobile phase of 3.5 mmol/L $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$; column temperature of 35 °C; flow rate of 0.6 mL/min; UV detector wavelength of 210 nm; and injection volume of 10 μL. Serial gradient standard solutions of butyric, lactic, acetic, and succinic acids were prepared to construct calibration curves by plotting mass concentration against the corresponding chromatographic peak area, and each organic acid was quantified using the external standard method.

## 1.8 Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

Three independent biological replicates were set up for shake-flask evaluation of engineered strains, shake-flask validation of representative mutants from sequential mutagenesis, single-factor optimization of medium components, and 5 L bioreactor scale-up experiments; experimental data are expressed as the mean ± sample standard deviation (mean ± SD). Experimental data plots were generated using Python and exported in scalable vector graphics (SVG) format. Schematic diagrams of the metabolic pathway, strain breeding workflow, and bioreactor setup were drawn as vector graphics based on the experimental design, and Adobe Illustrator was used to uniformly adjust the text, lines, and layout of the figures. The positive mutation rate (%) was calculated as the percentage of mutants exhibiting a higher butyrate titer than the corresponding parental control in primary screening relative to the total number of candidate mutants screened in that round (100 strains).

# 2 Results

## 2.1 Construction of the Butyrate Biosynthetic Pathway

Starting with the butyrate-producing strain *Bacillus megaterium* But-0, a series of chromosomal modifications was introduced using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homologous recombination. Genes associated with competing by-product pathways were sequentially deleted, and heterologous butyrate biosynthetic genes were integrated at the corresponding loci to reduce by-product formation and enhance butyrate production (Fig. 1A).

The lactate-forming branch was targeted first. Deletion of the lactate dehydrogenase gene *ldhA*, together with integration of the *Escherichia coli* acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase gene *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ at the same locus, generated strain But-1. Its butyrate titer reached 11.63 g/L, an increase of 10.44% over But-0 (10.53 g/L), while lactate concentration decreased by 75.97% from 4.30 to 1.03 g/L (Fig. 1B). Despite a 17.37% decrease in $\text{OD}_{600}$ from 14.97 to 12.37 (Fig. 1B), butyrate accounted for a higher proportion of the measured organic acids on a mass basis, increasing from 41.7% in But-0 to 47.7% in But-1 (Fig. 1C). However, acetate and succinate remained at 5.23 and 6.47 g/L, respectively, indicating that substantial accumulation of other competing by-products persisted.

To address acetate accumulation, the *ackA* gene encoding acetate kinase was deleted in But-1 and replaced at the same chromosomal locus with the *hbd*$^{\text{Ca}}$ gene encoding 3-hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydrogenase from *Clostridium acetobutylicum* (Fig. 1A). The resulting strain, But-2, reached a butyrate titer of 12.67 g/L, which was 8.88% higher than that of But-1. Concurrently, acetate concentration fell by 33.12% from 5.23 to 3.50 g/L, increasing the butyrate-to-acetate mass concentration ratio (B/A) from 2.22 to 3.62 (Fig. 1B). Lactate and succinate concentrations also declined from 1.03 to 0.60 g/L and from 6.47 to 4.73 g/L, respectively (Fig. 1B), raising the butyrate mass fraction to 58.9% (Fig. 1C), whereas $\text{OD}_{600}$ increased by 11.32% from 12.37 to 13.77 (Fig. 1B). Thus, this combined modification increased butyrate titer, reduced competing organic acid accumulation, and raised $\text{OD}_{600}$ relative to But-1.

The subsequent modification aimed to limit ethanol formation while extending the butyrate pathway. The *adhE* gene encoding aldehyde-alcohol dehydrogenase was deleted in But-2, and the *C. acetobutylicum crt*$^{\text{Ca}}$ gene encoding 3-hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydratase was integrated at this locus to obtain But-3 (Fig. 1A). Butyrate titer increased only marginally by 0.53% from 12.67 to 12.73 g/L, despite a 19.05% decrease in acetate concentration from 3.50 to 2.83 g/L (Fig. 1B). Lactate and succinate, however, rose from 0.60 to 0.97 g/L and from 4.73 to 5.17 g/L, respectively (Fig. 1B). While $\text{OD}_{600}$ increased by 10.17% from 13.77 to 15.17, the butyrate mass fraction remained essentially unchanged at 58.7% (versus 58.9% in But-2; Fig. 1B, C), indicating a limited improvement in butyrate accumulation from this step.

Subsequently, the pyruvate formate-lyase gene *pflB* was deleted in But-3 to target formate formation, and the *ter*$^{\text{Ca}}$ gene encoding *trans*-2-enoyl-CoA reductase from *C. acetobutylicum* was integrated at the same locus to generate But-4 (Fig. 1A). Butyrate titer increased by 6.28% from 12.73 to 13.53 g/L, and lactate and acetate concentrations decreased to 0.77 and 2.50 g/L, respectively (Fig. 1B). In contrast, succinate concentration rose by 30.97% from 5.17 to 6.77 g/L, reaching the highest level among strains But-0 through But-5 (Fig. 1B) and coinciding with a slight decline in the butyrate mass fraction from 58.7% to 57.4% (Fig. 1C). Meanwhile, $\text{OD}_{600}$ decreased by 6.81% from 15.17 to 14.13 (Fig. 1B). This pronounced accumulation of succinate provided a clear rationale for targeting the succinate-forming branch in the following round.

Accordingly, the fumarate reductase gene cluster *frdABCD* was deleted in But-4, and the *E. coli* acyl-CoA thioesterase II gene *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ was integrated at the same locus, yielding But-5 (Fig. 1A). Butyrate titer rose by 27.34% from 13.53 to 17.23 g/L, whereas succinate concentration dropped sharply by 64.04% from 6.77 to 2.43 g/L (Fig. 1B). Lactate and acetate concentrations further decreased to 0.57 and 2.10 g/L, respectively, with only a 2.12% increase in $\text{OD}_{600}$ from 14.13 to 14.43 (Fig. 1B). Across the first five engineering steps, the summed concentration of the measured by-products decreased by 65.38% from 14.73 g/L in But-0 to 5.10 g/L in But-5 (Fig. 1D). Meanwhile, the butyrate mass fraction increased from 57.4% in But-4 to 77.2% in But-5, exceeding that of the starting strain But-0 (41.7%) by 35.5 percentage points (Fig. 1C). The higher butyrate titer and markedly lower succinate and total by-product concentrations were thus achieved with minimal change in final $\text{OD}_{600}$ relative to But-4.

Five further deletion-and-integration modifications were introduced sequentially into But-5, retaining its previous modifications while targeting genes associated with acetate formation, alanine metabolism, oxaloacetate replenishment, and the methylglyoxal branch (Fig. 1A). Specifically, the *poxB*, *pta*, *alaT*, *ppc*, and *mgsA* genes were deleted, with *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$, *hbd*$^{\text{Ca}}$, *crt*$^{\text{Ca}}$, *ter*$^{\text{Td}}$ (from *Treponema denticola*), and *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ integrated at the respective loci to generate But-6. These modifications introduced second chromosomal copies of *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$, *hbd*$^{\text{Ca}}$, *crt*$^{\text{Ca}}$, and *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$, together with an additional *ter* homolog (*ter*$^{\text{Td}}$) alongside *ter*$^{\text{Ca}}$ at the *pflB* locus to further strengthen crotonyl-CoA reduction. The resulting strain, But-6, produced 22.07 g/L butyrate, representing increases of 28.05% over But-5 (17.23 g/L) and 109.49% over But-0 (10.53 g/L; Fig. 1B). Although $\text{OD}_{600}$ declined by 9.24% from 14.43 to 13.10 (Fig. 1B), But-6 exhibited the highest butyrate titer among the engineered strains and was therefore selected as the starting strain for three successive rounds of mutagenesis (ARTP, UV, and ARTP).

## 2.2 Enhanced Butyrate Production through Sequential ARTP–UV–ARTP Mutagenesis

To further improve butyrate production, strain But-6 was subjected to atmospheric and room-temperature plasma (ARTP) mutagenesis (Fig. 2A). In primary tube screening, candidates exhibiting a higher butyrate titer than the corresponding parental control were defined as positive mutants. Among 100 ARTP-derived candidates screened alongside the But-6 control (2.39 g/L), 16 positive mutants were identified, corresponding to a positive mutation rate of 16.00% (16/100; Fig. 2B,C). The highest-producing candidate in this screen, But-6A100, was selected for independent shake-flask validation. But-6A100 reached a butyrate titer of 27.73 g/L, an increase of 25.68% over But-6 (22.07 g/L), while final OD₆₀₀ increased by 70.74%, from 13.10 to 22.37 (Fig. 2D). But-6A100 was subsequently used as the parent for the next round of mutagenesis.

In the second round, But-6A100 was subjected to ultraviolet (UV) mutagenesis (Fig. 2A). Of 100 candidates evaluated in primary tube screening, 26 produced more butyrate than the But-6A100 parental control (3.09 g/L), yielding a positive mutation rate of 26.00% (Fig. 2B,C). The top-producing candidate, But-6B100, was selected for independent shake-flask validation and produced 31.93 g/L butyrate, a 15.14% increase over But-6A100 (27.73 g/L). In contrast, final OD₆₀₀ declined by 12.82%, from 22.37 to 19.50 (Fig. 2D). But-6B100 was then used as the parent for the third round of mutagenesis.

Using But-6B100 as the parent, a second ARTP treatment was conducted in the third mutagenesis round (Fig. 2A). Primary tube screening of 100 candidates against the But-6B100 parental control (3.59 g/L) identified 24 positive mutants, corresponding to a positive mutation rate of 24.00% (Fig. 2B,C). The highest-producing mutant in this screen, But-6C100, reached a butyrate titer of 34.93 g/L in independent shake-flask validation, an increase of 9.39% over its immediate parent But-6B100 (31.93 g/L). Final OD₆₀₀ also rose by 9.74%, from 19.50 to 21.40 (Fig. 2D). Across the three successive rounds of mutagenesis, the shake-flask butyrate titer of But-6C100 increased by 58.31% relative to the pre-mutagenesis parent But-6 (22.07 g/L), reaching 3.3-fold that of the original starting strain But-0 (10.53 g/L; Fig. 2D). Thus, the selected strains showed stepwise increases in shake-flask butyrate titer, although their final OD₆₀₀ values did not change in the same direction across rounds. But-6C100 was selected for subsequent optimization of fermentation medium components.

## 2.3 Optimization of Fermentation Medium Components for Butyrate Production

To further improve butyrate production by mutant strain But-6C100, the concentrations of four fermentation medium components were optimized sequentially in shake-flask cultures, with the selected concentration of each component carried forward to the next step. Yeast extract was examined first (Fig. 3A). Increasing its concentration from 2.5 to 5.0 and 7.5 g/L raised the butyrate titer from 26.30 to 34.33 and 36.17 g/L, respectively. The titer at 7.5 g/L was 37.52% and 5.34% higher than those at 2.5 and 5.0 g/L, respectively. However, further increasing yeast extract to 10.0 g/L lowered the titer to 34.20 g/L. Thus, 7.5 g/L yeast extract was selected for the subsequent optimization step.

With yeast extract maintained at 7.5 g/L, peptone was evaluated next (Fig. 3B). Butyrate titer increased by 12.21%, from 33.57 to 37.67 g/L, as peptone concentration rose from 2.5 to 5.0 g/L. The titer subsequently decreased to 36.77 and 35.27 g/L at 7.5 and 10.0 g/L peptone, respectively. Accordingly, the original basal-medium peptone concentration of 5.0 g/L, which gave the highest titer among the tested levels, was retained for subsequent optimization.

Corn steep liquor was then tested at concentrations of 4.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, and 12.0 g/L (Fig. 3C). As the corn steep liquor concentration increased from 4.0 to 10.0 g/L, the butyrate titer rose progressively from 24.30 to 38.57 g/L, an increase of 58.71%. The titer at 10.0 g/L was also 5.37% higher than that obtained at 8.0 g/L (36.60 g/L). Further supplementation to 12.0 g/L reduced the titer to 36.27 g/L. The highest titer within the tested range was therefore obtained with 10.0 g/L corn steep liquor, which was selected for the final step.

Finally, the effect of molasses concentration was examined (Fig. 3D). The butyrate titer rose from 36.53 to 38.30 and 40.47 g/L as molasses concentration increased from 2.5 to 5.0 and 7.5 g/L, respectively. At 7.5 g/L molasses, the titer was 10.77% higher than that at 2.5 g/L and 5.66% higher than that at 5.0 g/L; increasing molasses to 10.0 g/L reduced the titer to 38.23 g/L. The sequential single-factor experiments thus identified 7.5 g/L yeast extract, 5.0 g/L peptone, 10.0 g/L corn steep liquor, and 7.5 g/L molasses as the selected medium composition. With this formulation, But-6C100 produced 40.47 g/L butyrate in shake flasks, a 15.84% increase over its pre-optimization titer of 34.93 g/L (Fig. 3D). The selected concentrations of these four components were subsequently used for fed-batch fermentation in a 5 L bioreactor.

## 2.4 Butyrate Production by Fed-Batch Fermentation in a 5 L Bioreactor

To evaluate the butyrate production performance of mutant strain But-6C100 under controlled bioreactor conditions, fed-batch fermentation was conducted in a 5 L stirred-tank bioreactor using the optimized medium components determined in Section 2.3 (Fig. 4A). Based on the glucose feeding operation, the fermentation process was divided into a batch phase (0–24 h) and a feeding phase (24–48 h; Fig. 4B). During the batch phase (0–24 h), the initial glucose was rapidly consumed, with the residual glucose concentration dropping sharply from 51.13 to 1.83 g/L. Concurrently, $\text{OD}_{600}$ increased rapidly from 1.13 to 45.43, and the butyrate titer reached 33.43 g/L at 24 h (Fig. 4B), corresponding to an average volumetric productivity of 1.39 g/(L·h) during this phase (Fig. 4C). Continuous glucose feeding was initiated at 24 h, maintaining the residual glucose concentration between 1.83 and 3.93 g/L throughout the 24–48 h period, within the target feeding window of 1–5 g/L (Fig. 4B).

During the feeding phase (24–48 h), $\text{OD}_{600}$ continued to rise and reached a maximum of 52.70 at 36 h, when the butyrate titer was 46.63 g/L (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, although $\text{OD}_{600}$ gradually declined to 41.40 at 48 h, butyrate continued to accumulate and reached 58.63 g/L (a net increase of 12.00 g/L over the final 12 h; Fig. 4B). The average volumetric productivity over the feeding phase (24–48 h) was 1.05 g/(L·h) (Fig. 4C).

After 48 h of fermentation, the final butyrate titer, yield, and overall average volumetric productivity (0–48 h) of But-6C100 reached 58.63 g/L, 0.59 g/g, and 1.22 g/(L·h), respectively (Fig. 4B, C). Compared with shake-flask batch cultures in the basal medium (34.93 g/L) and the optimized medium (40.47 g/L), the final butyrate titer in the 5 L fed-batch bioreactor was higher by 67.84% and 44.89%, respectively (Fig. 4D), reaching 5.6-fold that of the initial starting strain But-0 (10.53 g/L; Fig. 5). These findings demonstrate sustained butyrate accumulation by But-6C100 under the fed-batch control strategy, including the period after $\text{OD}_{600}$ began to decline, providing a basis for further process scale-up.

# 3 Discussion and Conclusions

## 3.1 Balancing Competing Pathways and Butyrate Biosynthesis through Metabolic Engineering

The effects of successive chromosomal modifications on butyrate production were not proportional to the number of genes edited, indicating that the response to pathway engineering depended on the metabolic context of each strain. Disrupting a competing pathway may preserve precursors or reducing equivalents for butyrate synthesis, but it can also affect energy generation and growth. For example, deletion of the acetate kinase gene *ack* in *Clostridium tyrobutyricum* increased butyrate yield from 0.34 to 0.42 g/g glucose while reducing the specific growth rate from 0.21 to 0.14 h$^{-1}$ [15]. In another study, deletion of *ptb* did not substantially reduce butyrate formation but increased acetate production and lowered the butyrate-to-acetate ratio [16]. These observations illustrate why weakening competing reactions does not necessarily produce a proportional increase in butyrate titer. A similar distinction emerged during our strain construction. In But-1, deletion of *ldhA* combined with *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ integration reduced lactate accumulation from 4.30 to 1.03 g/L and increased butyrate titer by 10.44%. By contrast, the subsequent *adhE* deletion and *crt*$^{\text{Ca}}$ integration yielded only a 0.53% titer increase in But-3, accompanied by higher lactate and succinate concentrations than in its immediate parent. Because intracellular NADH, ATP, and carbon fluxes were not directly quantified, the product patterns are consistent with altered metabolic partitioning but do not establish a specific redox or energy mechanism.

The modification producing But-5 provided the clearest improvement in the distribution of measured organic-acid products. In But-4, succinate had increased to 6.77 g/L, whereas simultaneous deletion of *frdABCD* and integration of *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ in But-5 reduced succinate to 2.43 g/L and raised butyrate titer from 13.53 to 17.23 g/L, an increase of 27.34% (Fig. 1B). Across the first five engineering steps, the summed concentration of the measured by-products decreased from 14.73 to 5.10 g/L, while the mass fraction of butyrate among the measured organic acids increased from 41.7% in But-0 to 77.2% in But-5 (Fig. 1C,D). These changes are compatible with the annotated functions of FrdABCD in fumarate reduction to succinate and of TesB in hydrolyzing acyl-CoA thioesters, including butyryl-CoA, to release the corresponding acid and free CoA. However, the two modifications were introduced together; their individual contributions, any interaction between them, and the actual activities of the introduced enzymes were not determined. The distinction matters because the route used to release butyrate may also affect ATP conservation and cell growth. Wang et al. found that replacing an ATP-generating PTB/BUK route with a thioesterase-based route in engineered *Escherichia coli* altered both growth and volumetric butyrate productivity [8]. Thus, the favorable But-5 phenotype supports the combined engineering strategy, rather than proving that either modification alone removed a specific rate-limiting step.

Further chromosomal integration increased the butyrate titer of But-6 to 22.07 g/L, 109.49% above that of the starting strain But-0, although final $\text{OD}_{600}$ decreased from 14.43 in But-5 to 13.10 in But-6 (Fig. 1B). The additional edits targeted pathways associated with acetate, alanine, oxaloacetate, and methylglyoxal metabolism while introducing second chromosomal copies of key butyrate-pathway genes, including a second *ter* homolog. The improvement in titer despite the lower final optical density indicates that butyrate accumulation and biomass-associated optical density did not respond in parallel. This outcome is consistent with the broader need to balance pathway capacity, competing reactions, and host growth, but the contributions of individual loci in But-6 remain unresolved. Previous metabolic engineering studies have produced 28.4 g/L butyrate from glucose in mineral-salt medium using engineered *E. coli* and approximately 32.5 g/L with high butyrate selectivity using multiply engineered *Clostridium acetobutylicum* [8,17]. Those values provide context for alternative host and pathway designs, rather than a controlled ranking, because the organisms, substrates, pathway configurations, and culture conditions differ. The distinguishing feature of the present construction is the stepwise combination of competing-pathway modification with chromosomal integration of heterologous synthesis genes in an initially butyrate-producing *B. megaterium* strain.

## 3.2 Sequential Mutagenesis as a Complement to Targeted Engineering

Targeted pathway engineering was followed by phenotype-based screening to identify strains that accumulated more butyrate without requiring a predetermined genetic target. Across successive ARTP, UV, and ARTP treatments, the selected mutants But-6A100, But-6B100, and But-6C100 produced 27.73, 31.93, and 34.93 g/L butyrate in independent shake-flask validation, respectively (Fig. 2D). The final titer of But-6C100 was 58.31% higher than that of its pre-mutagenesis parent But-6 (22.07 g/L). Notably, the associated final $\text{OD}_{600}$ values did not increase monotonically: the first selected mutant showed an increase relative to But-6, the second showed a decrease relative to But-6A100, and the third showed a partial recovery relative to But-6B100. Selection for a higher butyrate titer therefore did not simply select for greater final optical density. Reports of ARTP-based strain improvement and physical mutagenesis in other microorganisms provide a methodological precedent for iterative screening and selection [18–21]. Nevertheless, neither the mutations responsible for the phenotype nor their effects on gene expression or intracellular metabolic flux were identified in the present study. Moreover, the successive use of different mutagens does not, by itself, demonstrate synergistic interactions between mutagenic treatments, and genetic stability cannot be inferred without serial-passaging experiments. These limitations distinguish the measured improvement in production phenotype from an established molecular mechanism. Genome resequencing, transcriptomic analysis, and stability testing would help identify and validate the changes responsible for the high-producing But-6C100 phenotype.

## 3.3 Medium Composition and Nutrient-Dependent Production

The responses to medium supplementation showed that increasing nutrient concentrations beyond the best-performing tested levels did not consistently improve butyrate production. With 7.5 g/L yeast extract, the butyrate titer reached 36.17 g/L, while 10.0 g/L yeast extract produced a lower titer. Similarly, corn steep liquor at 10.0 g/L yielded 38.57 g/L butyrate, but supplementation at 12.0 g/L reduced the titer (Fig. 3A,C). Yeast extract and corn steep liquor supply complex mixtures of nitrogenous compounds, vitamins, and other nutrients that can support microbial metabolism. Their effects may therefore reflect the balance between nutrient availability, cell growth, and product formation, although specific amino-acid uptake, cofactor levels, and osmotic effects were not measured here. The responses should consequently be interpreted as empirical concentration effects within the tested ranges, not as evidence of a particular intracellular regulatory mechanism or a global optimum. The final addition of 7.5 g/L molasses increased the shake-flask butyrate titer to 40.47 g/L (Fig. 3D). Unlike studies that use molasses as a major fermentable carbon source, including the engineered *C. tyrobutyricum* process that reached 45.71 g/L butyrate from untreated cane molasses [23], the present medium retained glucose as its principal carbon source and used molasses as a supplementary ingredient. Comparisons with published molasses-based fermentations [22,23] therefore highlight differences in feedstock strategy rather than demonstrating an equivalent degree of substrate utilization or an actual reduction in manufacturing cost. Future factorial or response-surface experiments could test interactions among the four additives and determine whether their selected concentrations remain preferable under different process conditions [27].

## 3.4 Interpretation of the 5 L Fed-Batch Fermentation

The fed-batch experiment demonstrated that the selected strain continued to accumulate butyrate after the initial batch phase, although the average volumetric productivity was lower during feeding. During 0–24 h, butyrate reached 33.43 g/L, corresponding to an average volumetric productivity of 1.39 g/(L·h). Following the start of glucose feeding at 24 h, measured residual glucose concentrations remained within the target range of 1–5 g/L, and a further 25.20 g/L butyrate accumulated during 24–48 h. The average volumetric productivity during this interval was 1.05 g/(L·h), compared with 1.22 g/(L·h) over the full 48 h (Fig. 4B,C). Importantly, the butyrate titer continued to rise while $\text{OD}_{600}$ decreased from its maximum of 52.70 at 36 h to 41.40 at 48 h. These time-course data establish continued net product accumulation during the late fermentation stage; they do not, on their own, establish whether the decline in optical density resulted from cell death, altered morphology, or other physiological changes. Nor do controlled pH and residual-glucose measurements demonstrate that product inhibition or acid stress was eliminated. In a separate study, He et al. compared batch and fed-batch fermentation of *Clostridium butyricum* at the same controlled pH and reported higher butyrate concentrations under the fed-batch regime [24]. The present comparison is different: the 5 L fed-batch experiment and the two shake-flask reference conditions also differed in glucose concentration, temperature, duration, and process control. Therefore, the observed increases of 67.84% and 44.89% over the basal- and optimized-medium shake-flask titers cannot be assigned exclusively to feeding or increased vessel size.

The final butyrate titer of 58.63 g/L in the 5 L bioreactor places the laboratory-scale performance of But-6C100 within the range of some reported high-titer *Clostridium* fermentations, although it does not establish comparable process efficiency or commercial feasibility. For example, Sjöblom et al. reported 58.8 g/L butyrate and a volumetric productivity of 1.9 g/(L·h) using *C. tyrobutyricum* in a 1 L fed-batch system supplied with sorghum hydrolysate and beet molasses [26]. Differences in host physiology, feedstock composition, cultivation mode, and bioreactor configuration limit direct comparisons of these values. In the present process, the decrease in average volumetric productivity from the initial batch stage to the feeding stage identifies late-stage carbon feeding and process control as priorities for further evaluation. Additional studies should use matched bioreactor batch and fed-batch controls, examine glucose-feed and aeration strategies, and document changing working volume and total substrate consumption. These measurements are also necessary for determining the substrate basis of the reported 0.59 g/g butyrate yield and for evaluating the process beyond laboratory scale.

## 3.5 Conclusions

Combining targeted metabolic engineering, successive physical mutagenesis and selection, sequential medium optimization, and laboratory-scale fed-batch fermentation increased the butyrate titer from 10.53 g/L in *B. megaterium* But-0 to 58.63 g/L in the final 5 L experiment (Fig. 5). The ten targeted chromosomal modifications yielded But-6 with a titer of 22.07 g/L (2.1-fold that of But-0); ARTP–UV–ARTP screening produced But-6C100 with a shake-flask titer of 34.93 g/L (3.3-fold); and medium optimization increased the corresponding shake-flask titer to 40.47 g/L (3.8-fold). Fed-batch cultivation of But-6C100 then achieved 58.63 g/L butyrate after 48 h (5.6-fold) and an overall average volumetric productivity of 1.22 g/(L·h). The combined approach thus improved the measured production phenotype in successive stages while revealing differences between growth-associated optical density and product accumulation. Further work is needed to resolve the genetic basis of the selected mutant's phenotype, quantify the separate contributions of individual pathway modifications, optimize the late fed-batch phase, and establish substrate- and volume-corrected production metrics before process scale-up.
