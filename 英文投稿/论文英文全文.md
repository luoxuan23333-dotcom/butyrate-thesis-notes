# 1 Materials and Methods

## 1.1 Materials

### 1.1.1 Bacterial strains

*Bacillus megaterium* CCTCC M2021167, obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC), was used as the starting strain and designated But-0. A series of engineered strains, But-1 through But-6, was sequentially constructed from But-0 by deleting host genes in competing metabolic pathways and integrating heterologous butyrate biosynthetic genes into the corresponding chromosomal loci. Using engineered strain But-6 as the parent, three successive rounds of mutagenesis—atmospheric and room-temperature plasma (ARTP), ultraviolet (UV), and ARTP—were conducted to obtain the mutant strains But-6A100, But-6B100, and But-6C100, respectively. The characteristics and sources of all strains used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Plasmid mini-prep, DNA gel extraction, and PCR product purification kits were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and the bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit was obtained from Tiangen Biotech (Beijing, China). Heterologous gene synthesis and PCR primer synthesis were performed by Yixin Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The main instruments included a PCR thermal cycler, electroporator, gel imaging system, electrophoresis apparatus, and benchtop high-speed refrigerated centrifuge (Eppendorf, Germany); a visible spectrophotometer (INESA Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China); an SBA-40D biosensor analyzer (Biology Institute, Shandong Academy of Sciences, China); a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA); an analytical balance and precision pH meter (Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland); a 5 L stirred-tank bioreactor (T&J Bio-engineering (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China); an ARTP mutagenesis breeding system (Wuxi Yuanqing Tianmu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China); and a UV mutagenesis apparatus.

## 1.2 Culture Media and Cultivation Conditions

### 1.2.1 Seed media

The solid seed medium used for strain activation contained (g/L): glucose, 30.0; yeast extract, 5.0; peptone, 5.0; and agar, 15.0. The liquid seed medium contained (g/L): glucose, 30.0; yeast extract, 5.0; and peptone, 5.0. Both media were prepared at natural pH and autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min (glucose was prepared and autoclaved separately, then aseptically mixed with the other components after cooling prior to inoculation). Luria–Bertani (LB) medium used for plasmid construction and preparation of mutagenized cell suspensions contained (g/L): peptone, 10.0; yeast extract, 5.0; and NaCl, 10.0 (with 15.0 g/L agar added for solid LB medium).

### 1.2.2 Fermentation medium

The basal liquid fermentation medium contained (g/L): glucose, 30.0; yeast extract, 5.0; and peptone, 5.0. The medium was used at its natural pH and autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min, with glucose sterilized separately and aseptically mixed with the other components before use.

### 1.2.3 Seed cultivation

Preserved strains were streaked onto solid activation medium and incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. A single colony was inoculated into a 100 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 25 mL of liquid seed medium and cultivated at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 16 h until reaching the logarithmic growth phase to serve as the seed culture.

### 1.2.4 Shake-flask fermentation

The seed culture was inoculated at 1% (v/v) into a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 100 mL of fermentation medium and cultivated at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 72 h. At the end of fermentation, samples were taken to measure the optical density at 600 nm ($\text{OD}_{600}$) and the concentrations of butyrate, lactate, acetate, and succinate in the fermentation broth.

## 1.3 Construction of Engineered Strains

### 1.3.1 Preparation of homologous recombination donor DNA fragments

Homologous recombination donor DNA fragments were constructed according to the target loci and integrated genes listed in Table 1. Taking the construction of engineered strain But-1 as an example, the upstream and downstream homology arms of the *ldhA* locus were amplified by PCR using the genomic DNA of the starting strain But-0 as the template, and the target gene fragment was amplified from a template carrying the *E. coli*-derived *atoB*$^{\mathrm{Ec}}$ gene. After ligating the target gene with expression regulatory elements including a strong promoter and a terminator, the upstream homology arm, the *atoB*$^{\mathrm{Ec}}$ expression cassette, and the downstream homology arm were assembled into a complete homologous recombination donor DNA fragment via overlap-extension PCR (fusion PCR). Donor DNA fragments for the remaining target loci were prepared using the same method.

### 1.3.2 Sequential construction of engineered strains

Chromosomal gene modifications were performed using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homologous recombination. The constructed donor DNA fragment was co-electroporated into competent host cells along with the pTargetF plasmid (carrying the sgRNA targeting the specific locus) and the pCas plasmid to achieve homologous recombination repair at the target site. Starting from But-0, the *ldhA* gene was deleted and replaced with *atoB*$^{\mathrm{Ec}}$ at the same locus to obtain strain But-1; subsequently, using But-1 as the parent, the *ackA* gene was deleted and replaced with *hbd*$^{\mathrm{Ca}}$ at the same locus ($\Delta ackA::hbd^{\mathrm{Ca}}$) to obtain strain But-2. Following the same procedure, the modifications $\Delta adhE::crt^{\mathrm{Ca}}$, $\Delta pflB::ter^{\mathrm{Ca}}$, and $\Delta frdABCD::tesB^{\mathrm{Ec}}$ were sequentially introduced to generate engineered strains But-3, But-4, and But-5, respectively. Finally, based on strain But-5, five additional gene deletion-and-integration modifications—$\Delta poxB::atoB^{\mathrm{Ec}}$, $\Delta pta::hbd^{\mathrm{Ca}}$, $\Delta alaT::crt^{\mathrm{Ca}}$, $\Delta ppc::ter^{\mathrm{Td}}$, and $\Delta mgsA::tesB^{\mathrm{Ec}}$—were sequentially completed to obtain engineered strain But-6.

### 1.3.3 Screening and verification of recombinant strains

Following electroporation and recovery, cell suspensions were spread onto selective agar plates containing the appropriate antibiotics. Single colonies were picked as candidate transformants and screened by colony PCR using verification primers located outside the homology arms of the target locus, combined with agarose gel electrophoresis and DNA sequencing to confirm gene deletion and heterologous gene integration. Verified positive clones were subjected to induction and cultivation at an elevated temperature to cure the editing plasmids pTargetF and pCas. After re-verifying the target locus sequence, the strains were preserved in glycerol stocks for the next round of genetic modification.

## 1.4 Sequential ARTP–UV–ARTP Mutagenesis and Strain Screening

### 1.4.1 Preparation of cell suspensions for mutagenesis

Strains But-6, But-6A100, and But-6B100 served as the parental strains for the three successive rounds of mutagenesis, respectively. Each parental strain was streaked from a glycerol stock onto an LB agar plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A fresh single colony was inoculated into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of liquid LB medium and cultured at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 10 h. The culture was centrifuged at 6,000 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended in sterile physiological saline, and counted using a bacterial counting chamber to adjust the cell concentration to approximately $1 \times 10^8$ CFU/mL prior to use.

### 1.4.2 ARTP and UV mutagenesis procedures

In the first round, starting strain But-6 was subjected to ARTP mutagenesis (Fig. 2A). A 10 μL aliquot of the prepared cell suspension was evenly spotted onto the center of a sterilized metal slide and placed in the ARTP mutagenesis apparatus. High-purity nitrogen was used as the working gas, with the radiofrequency power set at 120 W, treatment temperature at room temperature, gas flow rate at 10 SLPM, distance between the plasma emitter and sample at 5 mm, and exposure time of 30 s. Immediately after treatment, the metal slide was transferred into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 990 μL of sterile liquid seed medium and vortexed for 100 s to elute the cells. The eluate was then transferred into a 500 mL shake flask containing 50 mL of liquid seed medium and incubated at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 12 h. After appropriate serial dilution, 100 μL of the culture was spread onto solid medium plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, from which single colonies were picked as candidate mutants. In the second round, the high-yielding mutant But-6A100 selected from the first round was used as the parent for UV mutagenesis: 5 mL of the prepared cell suspension was placed in a sterile Petri dish (9 cm diameter) and irradiated for 30 s directly beneath a 30 W UV lamp at a distance of 30 cm; the treated suspension was serially diluted and plated in the dark throughout the process. In the third round, the selected mutant But-6B100 from the second round served as the parent for the second ARTP mutagenesis, following the same treatment parameters and cell recovery procedures as the first ARTP round.

### 1.4.3 Primary screening and shake-flask validation

After each round of mutagenesis, 100 candidate mutants were randomly picked from the plates (alongside one corresponding parental strain as a control) and inoculated into test tubes containing liquid fermentation medium for primary screening to determine butyrate titers. Candidates exhibiting a higher butyrate titer than the corresponding parental control were defined as positive mutants. The highest-producing mutants from the first two rounds, But-6A100 and But-6B100, served as the parents for the subsequent rounds, while But-6C100 was retained as the final selected mutant.

The starting strain But-6 and the three selected mutants (But-6A100, But-6B100, and But-6C100) were independently validated by shake-flask fermentation following the procedures described in Sections 1.2.3 and 1.2.4. Butyrate titers and $\text{OD}_{600}$ were measured at the end of fermentation, with three independent biological replicates performed for each strain.

## 1.5 Optimization of Fermentation Medium Components

Using mutant strain But-6C100 as the test strain and the basal fermentation medium (containing 30.0 g/L glucose) as the starting point, sequential single-factor experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of varying concentrations of yeast extract, peptone, corn steep liquor, and molasses on butyrate production (the optimal concentration determined for each preceding component was maintained as the baseline condition for the next component). Yeast extract, peptone, and molasses were each tested at four concentration gradients (2.5, 5.0, 7.5, and 10.0 g/L), whereas corn steep liquor was tested at five concentration gradients (4.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, and 12.0 g/L). All treatment groups were cultivated in shake flasks as described in Section 1.2.4, and butyrate titers were measured at the end of fermentation, with three biological replicates per treatment.

## 1.6 Fed-Batch Fermentation in a 5 L Bioreactor

Based on the single-factor optimization results, fed-batch fermentation of mutant strain But-6C100 was carried out in a 5 L stirred-tank bioreactor using the optimized medium components (7.5 g/L yeast extract, 5.0 g/L peptone, 10.0 g/L corn steep liquor, and 7.5 g/L molasses) (Fig. 4A). Following plate activation and seed cultivation, the culture was inoculated into the 5 L bioreactor at an initial $\text{OD}_{600}$ of 0.5. The working volume of the bioreactor was 2.5 L with an initial glucose concentration of 50.0 g/L, and cultivation was conducted at 37 °C with an agitation speed of 300 rpm and an aeration rate of 1.0 vvm. During fermentation, the broth pH was maintained at 7.0 by automated feeding of 4 mol/L NaOH solution. When the initial glucose was nearly exhausted at 24 h, a concentrated glucose solution was continuously fed using a feeding pump to maintain the residual glucose concentration within the target feeding window of 1–5 g/L. The fermentation lasted for 48 h, during which samples were aseptically withdrawn every 6 h to determine $\text{OD}_{600}$, residual glucose concentration, and butyrate titer.

## 1.7 Analytical Methods

Determination of $\text{OD}_{600}$: Fermentation broth was appropriately diluted, and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm using a visible spectrophotometer. Determination of residual glucose concentration: The supernatant obtained by centrifuging the fermentation broth was diluted 100-fold and analyzed using an SBA-40D biosensor analyzer.

Determination of organic acid concentrations: The concentrations of butyrate, lactate, acetate, and succinate in the fermentation broth were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Fermentation broth samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the collected supernatants were filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane prior to injection. Chromatographic conditions were as follows: a Bio-Rad Aminex HPX-87H organic acid analysis column (300 mm × 7.8 mm); mobile phase of 3.5 mmol/L $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$; column temperature of 35 °C; flow rate of 0.6 mL/min; UV detector wavelength of 210 nm; and injection volume of 10 μL. Serial gradient standard solutions of butyric, lactic, acetic, and succinic acids were prepared to construct calibration curves by plotting mass concentration against the corresponding chromatographic peak area, and each organic acid was quantified using the external standard method.

## 1.8 Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

Three independent biological replicates were set up for shake-flask evaluation of engineered strains, shake-flask validation of representative mutants from sequential mutagenesis, single-factor optimization of medium components, and 5 L bioreactor scale-up experiments; experimental data are expressed as the mean ± sample standard deviation (mean ± SD). Experimental data plots were generated using Python and exported in scalable vector graphics (SVG) format. Schematic diagrams of the metabolic pathway, strain breeding workflow, and bioreactor setup were drawn as vector graphics based on the experimental design, and Adobe Illustrator was used to uniformly adjust the text, lines, and layout of the figures. The positive mutation rate (%) was calculated as the percentage of mutants exhibiting a higher butyrate titer than the corresponding parental control in primary screening relative to the total number of candidate mutants screened in that round (100 strains).

# 2 Results

## 2.1 Construction of the Butyrate Biosynthetic Pathway

Starting with the butyrate-producing strain *Bacillus megaterium* But-0, a series of chromosomal modifications was introduced using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homologous recombination. Genes associated with competing by-product pathways were sequentially deleted, and heterologous butyrate biosynthetic genes were integrated at the corresponding loci to reduce by-product formation and enhance butyrate production (Fig. 1A).

To reduce lactate formation and reinforce the butyrate biosynthetic pathway, the lactate dehydrogenase gene *ldhA* was deleted in the starting strain But-0, and the *Escherichia coli*-derived acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase gene *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ was integrated at the same locus to generate But-1. Its butyrate titer reached 11.63 g/L, an increase of 10.44% over But-0 (10.53 g/L), while lactate concentration decreased by 75.97% from 4.30 to 1.03 g/L (Fig. 1B). Despite a 17.37% decrease in $\text{OD}_{600}$ from 14.97 to 12.37 (Fig. 1B), butyrate accounted for a higher proportion of the measured organic acids on a mass basis, increasing from 41.7% in But-0 to 47.7% in But-1 (Fig. 1C). Together, these results indicate that deletion of *ldhA* and integration of *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ substantially reduced lactate accumulation and increased butyrate titer. However, acetate and succinate still accumulated at relatively high concentrations of 5.23 and 6.47 g/L, respectively, indicating that the remaining competing pathways required further modification.

To reduce acetate formation and further reinforce the butyrate biosynthetic pathway, the *ackA* gene encoding acetate kinase was deleted in But-1 and replaced at the same chromosomal locus with the *hbd*$^{\text{Ca}}$ gene encoding 3-hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydrogenase from *Clostridium acetobutylicum* (Fig. 1A). The resulting strain, But-2, reached a butyrate titer of 12.67 g/L, which was 8.88% higher than that of But-1. Concurrently, acetate concentration fell by 33.12% from 5.23 to 3.50 g/L, increasing the butyrate-to-acetate mass concentration ratio (B/A) from 2.22 to 3.62 (Fig. 1B). Lactate and succinate concentrations also declined from 1.03 to 0.60 g/L and from 6.47 to 4.73 g/L, respectively (Fig. 1B), raising the butyrate mass fraction to 58.9% (Fig. 1C), whereas $\text{OD}_{600}$ increased by 11.32% from 12.37 to 13.77 (Fig. 1B). Taken together, these results indicate that deletion of *ackA* and integration of *hbd*$^{\text{Ca}}$ increased butyrate titer and final $\text{OD}_{600}$, reduced acetate accumulation, and improved butyrate selectivity relative to the other measured organic acids.

The subsequent modification aimed to limit ethanol formation while extending the butyrate pathway. The *adhE* gene encoding aldehyde-alcohol dehydrogenase was deleted in But-2, and the *C. acetobutylicum crt*$^{\text{Ca}}$ gene encoding 3-hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydratase was integrated at this locus to obtain But-3 (Fig. 1A). Butyrate titer increased only marginally by 0.53% from 12.67 to 12.73 g/L, despite a 19.05% decrease in acetate concentration from 3.50 to 2.83 g/L (Fig. 1B). Lactate and succinate, however, rose from 0.60 to 0.97 g/L and from 4.73 to 5.17 g/L, respectively (Fig. 1B). While $\text{OD}_{600}$ increased by 10.17% from 13.77 to 15.17, the butyrate mass fraction remained essentially unchanged at 58.7% (versus 58.9% in But-2; Fig. 1B, C). Despite the increase in final $\text{OD}_{600}$ and the further reduction in acetate accumulation, this modification produced only limited improvements in butyrate titer and its mass fraction among the measured organic acids.

To reduce formate formation and reinforce the butyrate biosynthetic pathway, the pyruvate formate-lyase gene *pflB* was deleted in But-3, and the *ter*$^{\text{Ca}}$ gene encoding *trans*-2-enoyl-CoA reductase from *C. acetobutylicum* was integrated at the same locus to generate But-4 (Fig. 1A). Butyrate titer increased by 6.28% from 12.73 to 13.53 g/L, and lactate and acetate concentrations decreased to 0.77 and 2.50 g/L, respectively (Fig. 1B). In contrast, succinate concentration rose by 30.97% from 5.17 to 6.77 g/L, reaching the highest level among strains But-0 through But-5 (Fig. 1B) and coinciding with a slight decline in the butyrate mass fraction from 58.7% to 57.4% (Fig. 1C). Meanwhile, $\text{OD}_{600}$ decreased by 6.81% from 15.17 to 14.13 (Fig. 1B). Although this modification increased butyrate titer, the accompanying pronounced succinate accumulation highlighted the need to target the succinate-forming branch in the following step.

To reduce succinate formation and reinforce the terminal steps of the butyrate biosynthetic pathway, the fumarate reductase gene cluster *frdABCD* was deleted in But-4, and the *E. coli* acyl-CoA thioesterase II gene *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ was integrated at the same locus, yielding But-5 (Fig. 1A). Butyrate titer rose by 27.34% from 13.53 to 17.23 g/L, whereas succinate concentration dropped sharply by 64.04% from 6.77 to 2.43 g/L (Fig. 1B). Lactate and acetate concentrations further decreased to 0.57 and 2.10 g/L, respectively, with only a 2.12% increase in $\text{OD}_{600}$ from 14.13 to 14.43 (Fig. 1B). Across the first five engineering steps, the summed concentration of the measured by-products decreased by 65.38% from 14.73 g/L in But-0 to 5.10 g/L in But-5 (Fig. 1D). Meanwhile, the butyrate mass fraction increased from 57.4% in But-4 to 77.2% in But-5, exceeding that of the starting strain But-0 (41.7%) by 35.5 percentage points (Fig. 1C). Together, these results show that the combined modification markedly reduced succinate and total by-product accumulation and improved both butyrate titer and product selectivity, while final $\text{OD}_{600}$ remained largely unchanged relative to But-4.

To further reinforce the butyrate biosynthetic pathway while concurrently targeting competing reactions associated with acetate formation, alanine metabolism, oxaloacetate replenishment, and the methylglyoxal branch, five additional deletion-and-integration modifications were sequentially introduced into But-5 while retaining its five preceding modifications (Fig. 1A). Specifically, the *poxB*, *pta*, *alaT*, *ppc*, and *mgsA* genes were deleted, with *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$, *hbd*$^{\text{Ca}}$, *crt*$^{\text{Ca}}$, *ter*$^{\text{Td}}$ (from *Treponema denticola*), and *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ integrated at the respective loci to generate But-6. These modifications introduced second chromosomal copies of *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$, *hbd*$^{\text{Ca}}$, *crt*$^{\text{Ca}}$, and *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$, together with an additional *ter* homolog (*ter*$^{\text{Td}}$) alongside *ter*$^{\text{Ca}}$ at the *pflB* locus to further strengthen crotonyl-CoA reduction. The resulting strain, But-6, produced 22.07 g/L butyrate, representing increases of 28.05% over But-5 (17.23 g/L) and 109.49% over But-0 (10.53 g/L; Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, $\text{OD}_{600}$ declined by 9.24% from 14.43 to 13.10 (Fig. 1B). These results indicate that although the additional modifications reduced final cell density, But-6 achieved a further increase in butyrate titer and had the highest titer among the engineered strains; it was therefore selected as the starting strain for successive ARTP–UV–ARTP mutagenesis.

## 2.2 Enhanced Butyrate Production through Sequential ARTP–UV–ARTP Mutagenesis

To further improve butyrate production, strain But-6 was subjected to the first round of atmospheric and room-temperature plasma (ARTP) mutagenesis (Fig. 2A). In primary tube screening, candidates exhibiting a higher butyrate titer than the corresponding parental control were defined as positive mutants. Among 100 ARTP-derived candidates screened alongside the But-6 control (2.39 g/L), 16 positive mutants were identified, corresponding to a positive mutation rate of 16.00% (16/100; Fig. 2B,C). The highest-producing candidate in this screen, But-6A100, was selected for independent shake-flask validation. But-6A100 reached a butyrate titer of 27.73 g/L, an increase of 25.68% over But-6 (22.07 g/L), while final $\text{OD}_{600}$ increased by 70.74%, from 13.10 to 22.37 (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that the first ARTP round yielded But-6A100 with higher butyrate titer and final $\text{OD}_{600}$ than the starting strain But-6. But-6A100 was therefore selected as the parental strain for the subsequent UV mutagenesis round.

To further improve butyrate production, But-6A100 was subjected to ultraviolet (UV) mutagenesis in the second round (Fig. 2A). Of 100 candidates evaluated in primary tube screening, 26 produced more butyrate than the But-6A100 parental control (3.09 g/L), yielding a positive mutation rate of 26.00% (Fig. 2B,C). The top-producing candidate, But-6B100, was selected for independent shake-flask validation and produced 31.93 g/L butyrate, a 15.14% increase over But-6A100 (27.73 g/L). In contrast, final $\text{OD}_{600}$ declined by 12.82%, from 22.37 to 19.50 (Fig. 2D). Despite its lower final $\text{OD}_{600}$, But-6B100 produced more butyrate than But-6A100 and was therefore selected as the parental strain for the subsequent ARTP mutagenesis round.

To further explore the strain's butyrate-producing potential, But-6B100 was subjected to a second ARTP treatment in the third mutagenesis round (Fig. 2A). Primary tube screening of 100 candidates against the But-6B100 parental control (3.59 g/L) identified 24 positive mutants, corresponding to a positive mutation rate of 24.00% (Fig. 2B,C). The highest-producing mutant in this screen, But-6C100, reached a butyrate titer of 34.93 g/L in independent shake-flask validation, an increase of 9.39% over its immediate parent But-6B100 (31.93 g/L). Final $\text{OD}_{600}$ also rose by 9.74%, from 19.50 to 21.40 (Fig. 2D). Across the three successive rounds of mutagenesis, the shake-flask butyrate titer of But-6C100 increased by 58.31% relative to the pre-mutagenesis parent But-6 (22.07 g/L), reaching 3.3-fold that of the original starting strain But-0 (10.53 g/L; Fig. 2D). Taken together, the primary-screening results and independent shake-flask validation showed progressive increases in butyrate production across the three ARTP–UV–ARTP mutagenesis rounds. Among the selected strains, But-6C100 exhibited the highest shake-flask butyrate titer and was therefore used for subsequent optimization of fermentation medium components.

## 2.3 Optimization of Fermentation Medium Components for Butyrate Production

To further improve butyrate production by mutant strain But-6C100, the concentrations of four fermentation medium components were optimized sequentially in shake-flask cultures, with the selected concentration of each component carried forward to the next step. Yeast extract was examined first (Fig. 3A). Increasing its concentration from 2.5 to 5.0 and 7.5 g/L raised the butyrate titer from 26.30 to 34.33 and 36.17 g/L, respectively. The titer at 7.5 g/L was 37.52% and 5.34% higher than those at 2.5 and 5.0 g/L, respectively. However, further increasing yeast extract to 10.0 g/L lowered the titer to 34.20 g/L. These results indicate that increasing yeast extract supplementation up to 7.5 g/L favored butyrate production within the tested range, with the highest titer obtained at this concentration. Therefore, 7.5 g/L yeast extract was selected for the subsequent optimization step.

To further evaluate the effect of peptone supplementation on butyrate production by But-6C100, peptone concentrations were examined with yeast extract maintained at 7.5 g/L (Fig. 3B). Butyrate titer increased by 12.21%, from 33.57 to 37.67 g/L, as peptone concentration rose from 2.5 to 5.0 g/L. The titer subsequently decreased to 36.77 and 35.27 g/L at 7.5 and 10.0 g/L peptone, respectively. The highest butyrate titer among the concentrations tested was obtained with 5.0 g/L peptone, whereas higher concentrations were less favorable for butyrate accumulation. The basal-medium peptone concentration of 5.0 g/L was therefore retained for subsequent optimization.

To determine the effect of corn steep liquor supplementation on butyrate production, five concentrations (4.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, and 12.0 g/L) were tested (Fig. 3C). As the corn steep liquor concentration increased from 4.0 to 10.0 g/L, the butyrate titer rose progressively from 24.30 to 38.57 g/L, an increase of 58.71%. The titer at 10.0 g/L was also 5.37% higher than that obtained at 8.0 g/L (36.60 g/L). Further supplementation to 12.0 g/L reduced the titer to 36.27 g/L. Within the tested range, increasing corn steep liquor supplementation to 10.0 g/L favored butyrate production, whereas a further increase to 12.0 g/L reduced the titer. The 10.0 g/L concentration was therefore selected for the subsequent step.

Finally, to further evaluate the effect of molasses supplementation on butyrate production, different molasses concentrations were tested (Fig. 3D). The butyrate titer rose from 36.53 to 38.30 and 40.47 g/L as molasses concentration increased from 2.5 to 5.0 and 7.5 g/L, respectively. At 7.5 g/L molasses, the titer was 10.77% higher than that at 2.5 g/L and 5.66% higher than that at 5.0 g/L; increasing molasses to 10.0 g/L reduced the titer to 38.23 g/L. These results indicate that 7.5 g/L molasses was the most favorable concentration for butyrate accumulation among those tested. The sequential single-factor experiments thus identified 7.5 g/L yeast extract, 5.0 g/L peptone, 10.0 g/L corn steep liquor, and 7.5 g/L molasses as the selected medium composition. With this formulation, But-6C100 produced 40.47 g/L butyrate in shake flasks, a 15.84% increase over its pre-optimization titer of 34.93 g/L (Fig. 3D). The selected concentrations of these four components were subsequently used for fed-batch fermentation in a 5 L bioreactor.

## 2.4 Butyrate Production by Fed-Batch Fermentation in a 5 L Bioreactor

To evaluate the butyrate production performance of mutant strain But-6C100 under controlled bioreactor conditions, fed-batch fermentation was conducted in a 5 L stirred-tank bioreactor using the optimized medium components determined in Section 2.3 (Fig. 4A). Based on the glucose feeding operation, the fermentation process was divided into a batch phase (0–24 h) and a feeding phase (24–48 h; Fig. 4B). During the batch phase (0–24 h), the initial glucose was rapidly consumed, with the residual glucose concentration dropping sharply from 51.13 to 1.83 g/L. Concurrently, $\text{OD}_{600}$ increased rapidly from 1.13 to 45.43, and the butyrate titer reached 33.43 g/L at 24 h (Fig. 4B), corresponding to an average volumetric productivity of 1.39 g/(L·h) during this phase (Fig. 4C). Continuous glucose feeding was initiated at 24 h, maintaining the residual glucose concentration between 1.83 and 3.93 g/L throughout the 24–48 h period, within the target feeding window of 1–5 g/L (Fig. 4B).

During the feeding phase (24–48 h), $\text{OD}_{600}$ continued to rise and reached a maximum of 52.70 at 36 h, when the butyrate titer was 46.63 g/L (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, although $\text{OD}_{600}$ gradually declined to 41.40 at 48 h, butyrate continued to accumulate and reached 58.63 g/L (a net increase of 12.00 g/L over the final 12 h; Fig. 4B). The average volumetric productivity over the feeding phase (24–48 h) was 1.05 g/(L·h) (Fig. 4C).

After 48 h of fermentation, the final butyrate titer, yield, and overall average volumetric productivity (0–48 h) of But-6C100 reached 58.63 g/L, 0.59 g/g, and 1.22 g/(L·h), respectively (Fig. 4B, C). Compared with shake-flask batch cultures in the basal medium (34.93 g/L) and the optimized medium (40.47 g/L), the final butyrate titer in the 5 L fed-batch bioreactor was higher by 67.84% and 44.89%, respectively (Fig. 4D), reaching 5.6-fold that of the initial starting strain But-0 (10.53 g/L; Fig. 5). These findings demonstrate sustained butyrate accumulation by But-6C100 under the fed-batch control strategy, including the period after $\text{OD}_{600}$ began to decline, providing a basis for further process scale-up.

# 3 Discussion and Conclusions

This study used *Bacillus megaterium* CCTCC M2021167 (But-0), a strain with an existing capacity for butyrate synthesis, as the starting strain and combined rational metabolic engineering with non-rational physical mutagenesis and strain selection. By progressively blocking competing by-product pathways and reinforcing the heterologous butyrate biosynthetic pathway through dual-copy integration, together with fermentation medium optimization and feeding control in a 5 L bioreactor, butyrate production increased in successive stages (Fig. 5). Through targeted modifications at ten loci, the butyrate titer of engineered strain But-6 increased from 10.53 g/L in But-0 to 22.07 g/L (2.1-fold). Subsequently, mutant strain But-6C100, obtained through successive ARTP–UV–ARTP mutagenesis and selection, produced 34.93 g/L butyrate in shake-flask fermentation (3.3-fold), and its shake-flask titer increased further to 40.47 g/L (3.8-fold) after single-factor medium optimization. Finally, following 48 h of fed-batch fermentation in a 5 L bioreactor, But-6C100 achieved a butyrate titer of 58.63 g/L (5.6-fold that of the starting strain), with a yield of 0.59 g/g and an average volumetric productivity of 1.22 g/(L·h) (Fig. 5). This study provides a new approach to efficient butyrate production by microbial fermentation and a reference for combining metabolic engineering with non-rational mutagenesis to develop *Bacillus* strains for the production of other organic acids.

Metabolic engineering strategies have been widely applied to improve butyrate production in industrial microorganisms, mainly through the construction and reinforcement of heterologous biosynthetic pathways, attenuation of competing metabolic branches, and regulation of precursor and cofactor balance [5–10,13–14]. However, the outcomes of different engineering strategies depend substantially on the original metabolic background of the host. Previous studies have shown that deletion of competing pathways is often accompanied by complex changes in cell growth and intracellular energy metabolism. For example, in *Clostridium tyrobutyricum*, inactivation of the acetate kinase gene (*ack*) increased butyrate yield from 0.34 to 0.42 g/g, but the specific growth rate decreased from 0.21 to 0.14 h$^{-1}$ [15]. Another study found that inactivation of the phosphotransbutyrylase gene (*ptb*) did not significantly decrease butyrate production; instead, acetate accumulation increased, resulting in a lower butyrate-to-acetate ratio [16]. This is because lactate formation consumes NADH, whereas acetate synthesis is coupled to ATP generation. Attenuation of these branches therefore directly changes the intracellular supply of reducing equivalents and energy, making it difficult to obtain a linear increase in butyrate production through single or paired gene modifications. Similar phenomena were observed during the stepwise strain construction in the present study. In But-1, deletion of *ldhA* and integration of *atoB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ reduced lactate concentration markedly from 4.30 to 1.03 g/L and increased butyrate titer by 10.44%, whereas deletion of *adhE* and integration of *crt*$^{\text{Ca}}$ in But-3 increased butyrate titer by only 0.53%. In contrast, deletion of *frdABCD* and integration of *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ in But-5 reduced succinate concentration markedly from 6.77 to 2.43 g/L, while butyrate titer increased from 13.53 to 17.23 g/L (an increase of 27.34%). Meanwhile, the total by-product concentration decreased from 14.73 g/L in the starting strain to 5.10 g/L, and the mass fraction of butyrate among the measured organic acids rose sharply from 41.7% to 77.2% (Fig. 1C,D). FrdABCD catalyzes the reduction of fumarate to succinate, whereas TesB can efficiently hydrolyze butyryl-CoA to release butyrate and free coenzyme A. The combined action of these two modifications effectively relieved the bottleneck in the terminal steps of butyrate synthesis while blocking a major competing branch. Compared with previous studies, the strain engineering strategy used here has three main features: (1) Distinct chassis characteristics: Wang et al. [8] introduced a heterologous pathway into *Escherichia coli*, which did not originally produce butyrate, and obtained 28.4 g/L butyrate in glucose-based mineral salts medium. In contrast, the *B. megaterium* chassis used in the present study already accumulated butyrate (10.53 g/L) and had favorable biosafety characteristics, making it more suitable for extensive metabolic remodeling at multiple loci. (2) Distinct rational engineering approaches: Jang et al. [17] primarily used multiple gene deletions to reduce by-product formation in *Clostridium acetobutylicum*, obtaining a butyrate titer of 33 g/L. The present study instead employed a paired, cumulative strategy of deleting competing genes at their native loci while simultaneously integrating heterologous biosynthetic genes. This approach attenuated the branches associated with lactate, acetate, formate, and succinate formation and reinforced the five key catalytic steps of the butyrate biosynthetic pathway through dual-copy integration of heterologous genes, increasing the butyrate titer of But-6 by 109.49% relative to the starting strain (to 22.07 g/L). (3) Integration of rational engineering with iterative compound mutagenesis: Multiple genomic modifications can impose a burden on host growth, as illustrated by the decrease in $\text{OD}_{600}$ associated with the deletion of the anaplerotic pathway gene *ppc* in But-6. Drawing on iterative ARTP–UV compound mutagenesis strategies [18–20] and physical irradiation-based strain selection [21], three successive rounds of mutagenesis were performed starting from the high-producing engineered strain But-6. By selecting a high-producing parent after each round, the representative mutants But-6A100, But-6B100, and But-6C100 achieved progressively higher shake-flask butyrate titers of 27.73, 31.93, and 34.93 g/L, respectively, with the final value representing an increase of 58.31% over But-6. This effectively combined the advantages of targeted rational design and broader phenotype-based selection. Taken together, future studies on genetic strain improvement could focus on two aspects: (1) constructing control strains carrying individual gene deletions or integrations to clarify the separate contributions of key modifications, such as *frdABCD* deletion and *tesB*$^{\text{Ec}}$ overexpression, to carbon flux distribution; and (2) using transcriptomic analysis or whole-genome resequencing to investigate the mechanisms underlying the high butyrate production of But-6C100, together with serial-passaging experiments to assess its genetic stability.

The nutritional composition of the fermentation medium and the process-control strategy in the bioreactor are also critical determinants of whether the butyrate production potential of the engineered strain can be fully realized. In the single-factor optimization experiments, increasing yeast extract and corn steep liquor concentrations to 7.5 and 10.0 g/L, respectively, increased butyrate titers to 36.17 and 38.57 g/L; however, further increases in their concentrations reduced butyrate production in both cases. Yeast extract and corn steep liquor are rich in peptides, amino acids, vitamins, and trace elements. Appropriate supplementation can promote cell growth and cofactor synthesis, whereas excessive nutrient levels may shift metabolism too strongly toward biomass accumulation or alter osmotic pressure, thereby reducing the formation of the target product. Previous studies have shown that nutrient requirements vary considerably among strains. For example, Huang et al. [22] used as much as 30 g/L corn steep liquor in *C. tyrobutyricum* fermentation, while Guo et al. [23] introduced sucrose-metabolism genes and obtained 45.71 g/L butyrate using high concentrations of untreated molasses as the main carbon source. By comparison, the present study added only 7.5 g/L molasses as a supplementary complex nutrient source, while glucose remained the main carbon source. The overall supplementation levels of yeast extract (7.5 g/L), peptone (5.0 g/L), and corn steep liquor (10.0 g/L) were also relatively moderate. This formulation not only increased the shake-flask butyrate titer to 40.47 g/L but also helped control industrial fermentation costs and reduce the burden of downstream product recovery. In the 5 L bioreactor scale-up experiment, controlling pH at 7.0 and continuously feeding glucose to maintain residual glucose within the target feeding window of 1–5 g/L effectively eliminated inhibition by high substrate concentrations and acid stress. This was consistent with the improvement in butyrate production through fed-batch fermentation reported by He et al. [24]. Notably, during the batch phase (0–24 h) of the 5 L fermentation, butyrate titer rapidly reached 33.43 g/L, with an average volumetric productivity of 1.39 g/(L·h). During the feeding phase (24–48 h), although $\text{OD}_{600}$ gradually decreased after reaching its peak at 36 h, butyrate continued to accumulate, with a net increase of 25.20 g/L and a final titer of 58.63 g/L. The average volumetric productivity during this phase was 1.05 g/(L·h), compared with 1.22 g/(L·h) over the entire fermentation. These results indicate that the feeding-control strategy enabled the strain to maintain substantial catalytic activity for butyrate production even after growth had entered the stationary phase and the early stage of decline. Compared with other representative butyrate fermentation processes, Ma et al. [11] and Jiang et al. [25] obtained 63.02 and 55.2 g/L butyrate, respectively, using immobilized *C. tyrobutyricum* cells in fibrous-bed bioreactors. Sjöblom et al. [26] used mixed feeding of sweet sorghum hydrolysate and beet molasses in a 1 L anaerobic system, obtaining 58.8 g/L butyrate and a volumetric productivity of 1.9 g/(L·h). In the present study, the engineered *B. megaterium* strain But-6C100 achieved a final butyrate titer of 58.63 g/L in a conventional 5 L stirred-tank bioreactor without requiring a complex cell-immobilization system or strictly anaerobic operation. This titer was comparable to those obtained in high-cell-density fermentations of anaerobic clostridia, demonstrating favorable potential for industrial scale-up. Taken together, future studies on fermentation process optimization could focus on two aspects: (1) using multifactorial experimental designs, such as response surface methodology (RSM) [27], to investigate interactions among nutrient components and explore the feasibility of replacing glucose with inexpensive molasses as the main carbon source; and (2) in view of the decrease in average volumetric productivity from 1.39 g/(L·h) during the batch phase (0–24 h) to 1.05 g/(L·h) during the feeding phase (24–48 h), further optimizing the glucose feeding rate and dissolved-oxygen control during the middle and late fermentation stages to shorten the fermentation cycle and increase overall productivity.
